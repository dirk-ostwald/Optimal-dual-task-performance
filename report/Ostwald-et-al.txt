Introduction

Voluntary task switching requires individuals to coordinate their actions across competing tasks. Even
under identical experimental conditions, individuals differ considerably in how they organize their
responses. The free concurrent dual-tasking (FCDT) paradigm provides an experimentally controlled
setting for investigating these differences while allowing participants to develop their own response
strategies. Two task streams remain concurrently visible, and participants decide which task to perform
on each response. Some individuals predominantly perform long sequences of one task before switching,
whereas others frequently interleave the two tasks. Still others exhibit response grouping, characterized
by closely spaced responses to both tasks following a longer processing interval. The general aim of
our work is to understand why different observers adopt these different response strategies and which
subjective constraints make their choices sensible courses of action.

Our modeling approach follows the rational analysis program as outlined in the project proposal (Brüning
et al., 2025). Rational analysis assumes that an observer’s choices can be understood as optimal with
respect to a particular representation of the decision problem and its subjective costs and benefits. For
the FCDT paradigm, relevant objectives include accurate and exhaustive task performance, avoiding
costly task switches, and maintaining a balance between the two tasks. Given a formal specification
of these objectives, dynamic programming provides algorithmic means to derive an optimal decision
policy. Conversely, observed response sequences can inform the subjective cost parameters under
which such a policy would be optimal. This inverse dynamic programming (Drugowitsch et al., 2012;
Kochenderfer et al., 2022; Ramachandran & Amir, 2007) perspective connects individual response
strategies to quantitative hypotheses about their determinants. To enable scientific inference about
these hypothesized determinants, we adopt the agentic behavioral modeling framework of Ostwald et al.
(2026). This framework combines a formal representation of the experimental task, an agent model of
the hypothesized cognitive mechanisms, and a probabilistic data model linking the agent’s decisions
to observed behavior. The resulting likelihood supports parameter estimation and model comparison,
allowing competing accounts of response strategy formation to be evaluated against empirical data.

In Brüning et al. (2025), we provided a preliminary proof of principle through generative simulations
of a simplified FCDT paradigm involving letter and digit categorization. The agent’s reward function
combined correct-response rewards with costs for switching tasks and accumulating task imbalance.
Varying the relative strength of these costs produced qualitatively different response sequences while
preserving accurate classification. High switching costs favored extended task blocks, whereas lower
switching costs yielded increasingly frequent task alternation, including choice patterns associated with
response grouping. These simulations demonstrated that differences in response organization could arise
from a common optimization architecture with different subjective cost parameters.

However, while these simulations provided an encouraging proof of principle for the rational analysis
of FCDT response strategies, their scope remained limited in both theoretical and empirical respects.




                                                   1
The finite-trial task formulation was solved through a discounted infinite-horizon approximation using
policy iteration, yielding stationary policies that did not account for the approaching end of an
experimental block. This approximation also introduced a preference for earlier over later rewards
without an independently established psychological justification in the present setting. Computational
demands further restricted the demonstration to four letters and four digits, simplifying the choice
environment relative to the experimental paradigm. The resulting account thus established the generative
plausibility of the proposed mechanisms, while their explanatory value for individual participants
remained unexamined, as the approach was neither applied to empirical response sequences nor used to
infer participants’ subjective cost parameters.

The present report takes a first step toward addressing these limitations by applying our rational analysis
approach to empirical FCDT data from Brüning et al. (2020). We extend the task formulation to
accommodate the richer experimental setting and derive optimal policies by backward induction for a
finite-horizon MDP without reward discounting. This allows decisions to reflect the remaining response
horizon while retaining a computationally tractable account of task performance. Embedding these
policies within the agentic behavioral modeling framework makes their explanatory value amenable to
statistical evaluation and enables estimation of individual subjective cost parameters from observed
response sequences.

The present report addresses the organization of response choices as a first step toward a temporal
account of FCDT performance. In future work on this project, we will extend this analysis within
a semi-Markov framework, deriving optimal policies jointly over task responses and waiting times to
capture both what participants choose and when they respond. This extension will relate response
strategies to the experimental deadline and the objective of maximizing correct-response throughput,
while accounting for the contribution of stimulus preview and preparation of the alternative task to
multitasking efficiency (Brüning et al., 2020). Doing so requires a more explicit representation of
processing time, cognitive effort, and concurrent task preparation within the agent’s subjective costs
and benefits. The current focus on response choices establishes the theoretical and statistical foundation
for this subsequent analysis of the timing and efficiency of task performance.

We then describe the model estimation and evaluation procedures. The Preliminary results section

Experimental data

For this report, we developed our approach for a subset of the data reported in Brüning et al. (2020).
Using the free concurrent dual-tasking paradigm, the authors investigated whether competition for
perceptual-cognitive and response-related resources affects how individuals organize their responses to
two concurrent tasks and how efficiently they perform them. Across two experiments, they examined
individual preferences for ‘blocking’, ‘switching’, and ‘response grouping’ under task combinations that
differed in their processing codes and response modalities. The original data and stimulus materials
are publicly available through the Open Science Framework. For the present work, we converted
the behavioral data from Experiment 1 to the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) format. For
implementational details, please refer to abm_preparation.py.

Experiment 1 comprised four dual-task combinations, each pairing a ‘memory-search’ task with a
‘classification’ task. The local BIDS dataset contains 49 participant records, whereas the article reports
48 recruited participants and an analyzed sample of 46 after exclusions (Brüning et al., 2020, p. 1415).
Both component tasks involved either spatial or verbal stimuli, yielding two combinations with the same
processing code and two with different processing codes. The ‘memory-search’ tasks required participants
to recognize previously memorized patterns or letter pairs, whereas the ‘classification’ tasks required
judgments about the rotation and reflection of three-dimensional objects or the parity of digit pairs.
All tasks were answered manually, allowing the authors to vary competition for perceptual-cognitive
resources while holding response modality constant. Experiment 2 extended this design by introducing
vocal responses to the verbal ‘memory-search’ task.


The PTD-RMC-FCDT paradigm

Here, we restrict our analysis to the combination of spatial ‘memory search’ and spatial ‘classification’
in Experiment 1. In this combination, participants decided whether a displayed pattern matched one
of three previously memorized target patterns and whether two three-dimensional objects differed
by rotation alone or by rotation and reflection. We refer to these component tasks as pattern target
detection (PTD) and rotation/mirror classification (RMC), respectively, and to their combination as
PTD-RMC. Participants organized their own task choices, with both task stimuli continuously visible.
Each response immediately advanced the corresponding task stream, while the stimulus of the other
task remained available for inspection. Responses were made with separate hands, with task-hand
assignment counterbalanced across participants. Data collection comprised three runs, each containing
two 120-second dual-task blocks, and stimuli were drawn from the respective stimulus sets without
immediate repetition (cf. Figure 1, left).

In more detail, the PTD task required participants to memorize three 2D patterns, presented as identical
pairs for 15 s before each task block. Subsequent probes were drawn from a set of 18 patterns, and
participants indicated whether the displayed pair matched one of the memorized targets. Targets and
non-targets each occurred with a probability of 50%. In the RMC task, four baseline 3D objects yielded
eight stimulus pairs, with the right object depicting either the left object rotated by 50 degrees or its
mirror image rotated by 50 degrees. Both types occurred with a probability of 50%, so chance accuracy
was 0.50 in both tasks (Brüning et al., 2020, pp. 1415–1416) (cf. Figure 1, right).

Regarding task allocation, participants were “instructed to not neglect or prefer one of the tasks
compared to the other” (Brüning et al., 2020, p. 1416). Regarding performance, they were “instructed
to maximize their amount of correct responses in the given time, that is, to perform the tasks according
to an optimum speed-accuracy balance” (Brüning et al., 2020, pp. 1416–1417). No particular switching
strategy was prescribed. At the end of every block, feedback was displayed for 5 s and reported,
separately for each task, the number of performed trials and the number and percentage of correct
responses (Brüning et al., 2020, p. 1417). Participants thus received information about both response
throughput and accuracy for each task, rather than a single combined performance score. In addition to
a fixed payment or course credit, they received a monetary bonus for each correctly answered stimulus,
which could amount to EUR 10 in total (Brüning et al., 2020, p. 1415). The article does not specify the
payment per correct response or a numerical penalty for task imbalance or switching.

In the authors’ analyzed sample of 46 participants, the classification under high resource competition,
which included this task combination, identified approximately 25 ‘blockers’, 15 ‘switchers’, and six
‘response groupers’, illustrating the response strategies described in the Introduction (Brüning et al.,
2020, Figure 3). For implementational details, please refer to abm_description.py.

Agentic behavioral model

Following Ostwald et al. (2026), we distinguish three components of an agentic behavioral model: a task
model, an agent model, and a data model. The task model describes the experimental environment by
specifying its possible states, the actions available to participants, and the consequences of those actions
for subsequent states and performance outcomes. It thus provides a mathematical formulation of the
rules governing the experiment. The agent model describes a computational approach to performing the
task and serves as a hypothesis about the cognitive mechanisms underlying participants’ behavior. It
specifies the agent’s knowledge of the task, its objectives, and the processes through which these inform
its decisions. The data model connects this account to observed behavior by embedding the task and
agent models within a statistical framework. This connection enables the estimation of model parameters
and the comparison of competing accounts of task performance. Together, the three components make
explicit the experimental conditions, the hypothesized cognitive processes, and the assumptions through
which these hypotheses become amenable to empirical evaluation.


Notation

In general, we use time-indexed lower-case letters for discrete-time random variables and non-indexed
lower-case letters for the values they take. On occasion, a time-indexed lower-case symbol denotes a
function. This use is clear from context and an explicit function definition. Thus, statements such as
𝑥𝑡 = 𝑥 mean that the random variable 𝑥𝑡 takes on the value 𝑥. The corresponding upper-case letter
denotes the set of values a random variable can take, with a time index added when this set varies over
time. Collections of random variables are indicated by colon notation, i.e., for integer indices 0 ≤ 𝑡 ≤ 𝑡′ ,
write
                                         𝑥𝑡∶𝑡′ ∶= (𝑥𝑡 , 𝑥𝑡+1 , … , 𝑥𝑡′ ).                                 (1)

A collection with an end index smaller than its start index is empty. We use 𝑝 for probability mass
functions and overload it for joint, marginal, and conditional mass functions, as identified by its arguments.
Expectations are used only when the relevant variable is integrable. Conditional probabilities and
conditional expectations given a value are used only when that value has positive probability, unless
a particular version of the conditional distribution or expectation has been specified (we understand
a version as a concrete assignment of a conditional distribution or expectation to every conditioning
value, including zero-probability values at which the joint distribution does not determine it. Different
versions agree at positive-probability values but may differ at zero-probability values).

To keep probability calculations compact, we suppress explicit assignments of random variables to their
values inside probability expressions and conditional expectations. Explicit assignments are retained
when needed to specify particular component events, transformed values, or a value denoted by a distinct
summation or maximization variable.

Time indices

The trial- and action index set is
                                              𝐽 ∶= {0, 1, … , 𝑛},                                    (5)

where 𝑛 is the observed action count within the fixed time window. It may vary across participants
and blocks but is held constant in each task model. Actions are selected at stages 𝑡 = 0, … , 𝑛 − 1, and
stage 𝑛 is the horizon boundary. Note that 𝑛 denotes the horizon represented by 𝑇 in S1. Here, the

self-loops introduced below complete the conditional state distribution and do not determine when the
block ends.

Initial-state distribution

At block onset, the stimulus label and identifier pairs of the two task streams are assumed to be mutually
independent. Each stimulus label is uniformly distributed over {0, 1}, and, conditional on its label, each
identifier is uniformly distributed over the corresponding set 𝐼𝑗,𝑙 . A discrete uniform distribution on a
finite set assigns equal probability to every element of that set and zero probability to all other values.

State-action conditional state distribution

The state-action conditional state distribution describes how a response changes the experimental state.
Following S1, we use 𝑠𝑡 , 𝑎𝑡 , and 𝑠𝑡+1 for the current state, action, and successor-state values, suppressing
explicit assignments inside probabilities. The distribution is Markovian and time homogeneous: the
next state depends on these values without additional dependence on the preceding history or decision
stage.

Boundary convention

The conditional distribution is prescribed for every state-action pair in 𝑆 × 𝐴, including combinations
that cannot occur before the horizon boundary. At |𝑠4𝑡 | = 𝑛, we let either action leave the state

would otherwise take it outside 𝐵. Starting from 𝑆0 , absolute imbalance can increase by at most one
per action, so this boundary cannot be reached before the final action. No further action is selected
then. Consequently, the self-loops complete the mathematical specification without affecting trajectories
over the 𝑛 decision stages. The horizon determines when the block ends, rather than the boundary
convention.

Note that the task reward is fully determined once the current state and selected action are specified.
The state identifies the correct response for each task, and the action identifies both the selected task

outcome vector 𝜚(𝑠, 𝑎) and probability zero to every other reward value.

This also determines the joint conditional distribution of the next state and reward, as introduced in

trials. The outcome vector changes with the selected task and response accuracy, and rewards can be
uncertain when the current state or selected action is not given.

The instructions and monetary incentives described for the PTD-RMC-FCDT paradigm motivate the
performance quantities recorded by this reward. They do not specify numerical costs for task imbalance
or switching. Accordingly, 𝜚 records task performance without assigning subjective values to these
outcomes or costs to particular response strategies. Note that this vector-valued task reward differs from
the scalar reward assumed in S1. The agent’s scalar subjective reward, which specifies its optimization
goals, is introduced in the subjective reward function section.

Subjective reward function

The subjective reward expresses the agent’s preference for correct responses and balanced task completion,
together with the cost it assigns to switching between tasks. We represent these optimization goals by

Task accuracy

The accuracy component rewards correct responses in either task. It is defined by (𝑠, 𝑎) ↦ 𝜌(𝑠, 𝑎),

The balance component expresses a preference for completing similar numbers of trials in the two tasks.

Its linear form and weight are modeling assumptions.

MDP reduction

The agent combines the task dynamics with its subjective reward to form an unreduced internal MDP,
denoted by ℳ𝛽,𝜎 . This decision problem uses the scalar subjective reward rather than the task’s
vector-valued performance outcomes. We reduce it in three steps, retaining the information needed to
derive optimal decisions. The argument below explains each step intuitively. The following theorem
defines both MDPs and states the preservation of optimal values and optimality under action lifting.

For the intermediate reductions, we consider the regular state space 𝑆+ introduced in the task state-space
section. The complete state space remains 𝑆 = 𝑆0 ∪ 𝑆+ . The reduced initial state and the lifting of
initial decisions are included below.




                                                        19
Removing stimulus identifiers

The first step removes the stimulus identifiers while retaining both stimulus labels, the previously
selected task, and the imbalance. None of the subjective reward components depends on the identifiers.
They do, however, affect the state-action conditional state distribution, because the exclusion of direct
repetitions removes the current identifier from the possible successors in the selected task stream.

The crucial distinction is that this exclusion changes which identifier can appear next, without changing
the probabilities of the next label. Away from the imbalance boundary, either label occurs with
probability 1/2, independently of the current identifier, as established in the task dynamics section.
This relies on 𝑛𝑗,𝑙 ≥ 2, which holds for every task and label in the present model. Conditional on the
new label, the identifier determines only which particular stimulus is excluded from that label class.

We can therefore group task states with the same two labels, previous task, and imbalance into a reduced
state class. Summing the successor-state probabilities within each class removes their dependence on
the current identifiers. The immediate reward and the probability of every reduced successor class
consequently depend only on the retained state components. At the imbalance boundary, the self-loop
also preserves this property. This motivates the mapping

                                𝜙 ∶ 𝑆+ → 𝑆𝜙 , 𝑠 ↦ 𝜙(𝑠) ∶= (𝑠1,1 , 𝑠2,1 , 𝑠3 , 𝑠4 ) .                       (41)

Its reduced state space is
                                            𝑆𝜙 ∶= {0, 1}2 × 𝑇 × 𝐵.                                         (42)

Thus, removing identifiers preserves both the rewards and the state-action conditional state distribution
needed for planning at the level of these reduced states.

Excluding incorrect actions

The second step restricts planning to the correct action for each task. As specified in the task action-set
section, an action contains a task choice and a binary response. For a given task 𝑗, the correct action
(𝑗, 𝑠𝑗,1 ) and the incorrect action (𝑗, 1 − 𝑠𝑗,1 ) produce the same state-action conditional state distribution.
Both affect the same task stream and have identical consequences for the previous-task component and
task imbalance. They also incur the same imbalance and switching costs.

Their accuracy rewards differ by exactly one unit in favor of the correct action. Since their next-state
distributions coincide, their expected future rewards are identical under any common continuation
policy. The correct action therefore yields an expected total reward exactly one unit higher at every
decision stage. The incorrect action is thus strictly dominated by the correct action for the same task,
an instance of action dominance under identical transition probabilities (see Keller & Eyerich (2012),
Definition 1).




                                                        20
Excluding these dominated actions preserves the optimal value. The two remaining choices can be
represented by abstract task actions,
                                                 𝐴𝜒 ∶= {1, 2}                                         (43)

where 𝑗 means selecting the correct action for Task 𝑗. This abstraction retains the task choice while
leaving its state-dependent response component to the action-lifting map introduced below.

Once each abstract action selects a correct response, its accuracy reward is always one, irrespective of
the current stimulus labels. The imbalance and switching costs already depend only on the selected task,
the previous task, and the current imbalance. These latter state components also evolve independently
of the stimulus labels. The labels can therefore be removed from the planning state without changing
the rewards or the probabilities of its reduced successors. This gives the regular reduced state space

                                                𝑆𝜒,+ ∶= 𝑇 × 𝐵                                         (44)

The intermediate state retains the component indices of the task state. The stimulus labels remain part
of the task state. They are unnecessary for choosing between tasks under this reward function, but
remain necessary for translating a task choice into the corresponding correct action.

Including the initial state gives the complete reduced state space

Outlook

The next step is to extend the present choice-based account to the temporal organization of behavior.
The current model conditions on the observed number of responses within each 120-second block. It
explains which task participants selected at successive decision stages, but not how their response times
determined the number and temporal spacing of those decisions. A more complete model should treat
both the selected response and the time at which it is made as outcomes of the agent’s policy. This
would allow the analysis to address the central performance objective of the FCDT paradigm: using the
limited block time efficiently to produce as many correct responses as possible.

A natural framework for this extension is a finite-time semi-Markov decision process. Decisions would
occur at random epochs, and each action would jointly determine a distribution over the duration until
the next response and the successor task state. The corresponding semi-Markov kernel would therefore
describe both what happens next and how much of the 120-second horizon is consumed. The agent’s
effective control state would include the physical task state and the remaining time. Its policy could
then optimize the task-response decision together with a waiting-time decision. Responding sooner
would preserve time for later trials, whereas waiting could improve preparation or response accuracy.
The resulting optimality equation would be a renewal-type Bellman equation indexed by remaining
calendar time rather than the finite-stage recursion used in the present model. This development can
draw on the standard semi-MDP framework and its finite-horizon extensions (Ascione & Cuomo, 2022;
Huang & Guo, 2011; Jewell, 1963; Puterman, 1994, Chapter 11; Tijms, 2003, Chapter 7).

Several technical points from this literature will be important. The model must specify what happens
when a response would occur after the block deadline and whether a response exactly at the deadline



                                                                32
receives a reward. Nonexplosion and integrability conditions are required to ensure that the expected
total reward is well defined. If response times are represented on a discrete temporal grid with
strictly positive durations, the problem can be solved by backward induction on the remaining time.
For continuous response times, the value function becomes a function of continuous remaining time.
Numerical implementation would then require a time grid, quadrature, or an approximation of the
duration distributions by discrete distributions. The state-space reductions used in the present report
would also have to be reconsidered because response times and accuracy may depend on the currently
displayed stimuli or on their processing history.

The temporal extension should be accompanied by a cognitively richer account of subjective reward.
The present reward function represents correct responding, task balance, and a fixed cost of switching.
This provides a useful first approximation, but it does not directly represent the processing demands
that the experiment was designed to manipulate (Brüning et al., 2020). A future reward function
could include costs of perceptual processing, response preparation, and maintaining or updating task
representations. These costs need not be constant across tasks or trials. They could depend on stimulus
properties, the time devoted to processing, the amount of preparation already completed, and whether
processing resources are shared by the two tasks.

Importantly, frequent switching need not be represented solely as costly. In the FCDT paradigm, both
stimuli remain continuously visible, so the stimulus of the unselected task can be previewed while the
participant prepares or executes a response to the selected task. This preview may reduce the time or
processing effort required for a subsequent response. Frequent alternation could therefore provide an
efficiency benefit when preparation of the alternative task is possible, even if switching itself carries a
cost. The same mechanism may help explain response grouping, where a longer processing interval is
followed by two closely spaced responses. A richer state representation could track the preparation or
accumulated evidence associated with each task stream, allowing the model to express the trade-off
between acting immediately, waiting for further processing, repeating the current task, and switching to
a previewed task.

This extension is ambitious but builds directly on the present framework. The current finite-horizon
MDP establishes the task dynamics, the relation between subjective costs and task choices, and the
likelihood-based connection to participant behavior. Adding semi-Markov timing and processing variables
would make it possible to evaluate response choice, response time, and task efficiency within a common
optimization model. Once this extension has been specified and implemented, its face validity should
be examined through simulations of the principal behavioral patterns, followed by model-recovery and
parameter-recovery analyses. These steps would provide a more informative basis for assessing whether
individual FCDT strategies reflect different subjective costs, different processing constraints, or different
uses of stimulus preview and concurrent preparation under the rational analysis framework.

S1 Finite dynamic programming

In these notes, we review the derivation of optimal policies for Markov decision processes with finite
time horizon and finite state, action, and reward spaces. We make the additional assumption of no
terminal states. We first establish the basic terminology by defining Markov decision processes, decision
functions and policies, and policy-dependent Markov decision processes. We then establish conditional
independence properties and introduce the return, value function, and action-value function, together
with their recursive relationships. Finally, after defining optimal value functions and optimal policies,
we prove the central result in Theorem 9, which shows how a deterministic Markovian optimal policy
can be constructed algorithmically by backward induction. The material covered here is discussed in
Puterman (1994, Chapter 2 and 4), Bertsekas (2005, Volume I, Chapter 1), Powell (2022, Chapter 14),
Krishnamurthy (2025, Chapter 7), and Rao & Jelvis (2022, Chapter 4 and 5).

Policy-dependent value and action-value functions

The fundamental goal of policy optimization is to maximize the expected cumulative reward over
the remaining decision stages, which may comprise all decision stages when starting at the initial
time. In MDP theory, this objective is formalized through the return, the value function, and the
action-value function. The return is the sum of future rewards, optionally discounted to give less weight
to rewards received later. The value function gives the expected return when starting from a given state
and following a policy, whereas the action-value function gives the expected return when additionally
prescribing the first action. Together, these quantities allow us to evaluate policies and compare the
actions available at each state and time.

Their recursive relationships, as stated in Theorem 5, Theorem 7, and Theorem 8, then provide the
basis for dynamic programming and the application of Bellman’s principle of optimality: whatever the
initial state and first action, the remaining decisions must form an optimal policy for the state reached
and the decision stages that remain. Accordingly, an optimal action maximizes the expected immediate
reward plus the discounted optimal value of the next state. For a finite horizon, this principle leads to a
backward induction procedure that constructs an optimal policy by solving the decision problem at
each time in reverse order.

Optimal value functions and policies

We now turn to the central task of these notes: establishing the existence of an optimal policy and
showing how to construct it. Building on the value functions and recursive relationships developed
above, we first define optimal value functions and optimal policies. The main result then shows that
backward induction yields a deterministic Markovian policy that attains the optimal value at every
state and time (Theorem 9). Finally, we introduce the optimal action-value function and relate it to the
optimal value function.

Historically, the backward-induction construction in Theorem 9 traces to Bellman’s finite-stage stochastic
recursion (Bellman (1954, Section 4)), with its finite-state, finite-action form explicitly given in Bellman
(1957, equation (1.1) and Section 2). Earlier contributions to sequential decision theory include Arrow
et al. (1949), whose work provided part of the foundation for this development.

S2 Experimental statistics validation

Purpose and validation design

The state transition model specifies how a new stimulus is generated after the participant selects one of
the two tasks. Its observable implications can therefore be examined without fitting an agent model.
We used the experimental PTD-RMC data to estimate the transition frequencies of the stimulus labels
and identifiers, and compared them with the corresponding frequencies obtained from simulations of the
full MDP. The aim was not to reproduce participants’ task-allocation behavior. It was to determine
whether the state statistics generated by the model resemble those implemented in the experiment.

The comparison was restricted to PTD and RMC, which are the two tasks of the paradigm analyzed
in the main text. All 49 participants were included. Their original run and block structures were
retained, as were the task-specific stimulus catalogues, the participant- and block-specific PTD target
sets, the fixed RMC categories, and the number of trials observed in each block. This design matching
is important because pooled frequencies can otherwise differ merely because target assignments or block
lengths differ between an experimental and a simulated sample.

For the simulation, actions were generated exclusively by A0. At every trial, A0 selects uniformly from
the four task-response actions. No fitted parameters and no optimal-policy computations enter this
validation. The selected action determines which stimulus stream is advanced, while the full MDP
generates the successor state according to its transition distribution. This provides a direct simulation
of the model component under examination and is much faster than generating the same states through
A1.

The use of A0 is also conceptually appropriate. Conditional on the selected task, the transition law for
the new label and identifier does not depend on why that task was selected. Different action policies
change how often PTD or RMC is sampled in a finite block, but they do not change the corresponding
conditional stimulus distribution. With sufficiently many transitions, the estimated state statistics
should therefore not depend on whether actions were generated by A0, A1, or a participant. Uniform
random actions provide balanced exposure to both streams without importing a behavioral model into
the validation.

The observed and simulated data were processed by the same counting function. Thus, differences
between the two sets of figures cannot arise from using different definitions, recodings, or normalization
procedures. A fixed random seed makes the simulated comparison reproducible. The simulation remains
one finite realization of the model, so exact numerical equality is neither expected nor required.




                                                   56
Transition reconstruction and frequency estimates

Each data row records the state visible before an action. A selected-stream transition was reconstructed
by pairing this state with the next state in the same participant, run, and block. Transitions were never
formed across block boundaries or across gaps in the trial index. The terminal action of a block has no
recorded successor and consequently contributes no transition. A missing stimulus in the unselected
stream does not exclude an otherwise known update of the selected stream.

Stimulus labels were recovered from the experimental design rather than from participants’ responses.
For PTD, the three targets were read separately for each participant and block. The remaining 15
patterns received label 0. For RMC, the four rotation-only stimuli and the four mirrored-and-rotated
stimuli form fixed label classes. Responses were retained when the label-frequency panels were split into
No and Yes actions. They were pooled for the identifier matrices because the transition law depends on
the selected task but not on the response component of its action.

Simulation comparison and interpretation

The validation simulation reproduced every PTD-RMC block in the experimental design. Each simulated
block used its original stimulus catalogue, PTD target set, RMC category assignments, and observed
number of trials. The initial state and every successor state were sampled from the full MDP. Only the
action generator was simplified to A0. The resulting simulated cohort has the same participants and
blockwise observation opportunities as the experimental cohort, apart from the random allocation of
actions and the terminal transition that is unobserved in both data sources.

The comparison is descriptive and focuses on the probability patterns that are visible in the pooled
data. For PTD, the experimental and simulated label frequencies are both concentrated near 1/2. The
identifier matrices show the same exclusion of direct repetitions and the same division between target
and non-target supports. The target-successor panels also reproduce the expected probabilities over the
three target ranks after the block-specific target sets have been aligned. Figure S1A and Figure S1B
show the experimental and simulated PTD results.

The RMC comparison yields the same conclusion. Both data sources preserve the fixed four-by-four
label partition. A label change leaves four eligible successor identifiers, while retaining the same label
leaves three because the current identifier is excluded. The corresponding probabilities are therefore
concentrated near 1/4 and 1/3, respectively. The remaining variation is consistent with finite counts in
the individual conditioning rows. Figure S1C and Figure S1D show these results.

Agreement in these pooled summaries validates the experimentally relevant stimulus-generation statistics
of the full MDP. It does not by itself test every conditional independence statement at the level of
complete states. Many fully specified states are sparsely observed, and pooling can conceal small
context-specific discrepancies. It also does not validate A0 as a behavioral account. A0 is used only to
create action-independent exposure to the transition kernel. Participant behavior and the comparison of
A0 with A1 remain separate questions addressed by the agent-model analysis.

The analysis applies the same counting function to observed and simulated trials. The associated nu-
merical observed-simulated discrepancies are exported with the validation results. For implementational
details, please refer to abm_validation.py.

S3 Implementational notes

Successor indices for backward induction

The reduced transition distribution assigns probability one to a single successor for each state-action
pair. We exploit this by storing the reduced states as rows of the array mdp_chi.S and recording the
row index of each successor in mdp_chi.k. Each row of mdp_chi.k has two entries, corresponding to
the two abstract task actions. State row indices are distinct from the decision-stage index 𝑡. Row zero
represents the initial state, while the remaining rows enumerate the regular states in 𝑇 × 𝐵.

Write 𝑠(𝑖) for the state in row 𝑖, let 𝑘(𝑖, 𝑎) denote the row of its unique successor under action 𝑎, and
store continuation values as 𝑉𝑡 (𝑖) ∶= 𝑣𝑡 (𝑠(𝑖) ). The expectation appearing in backward induction then
satisfies

"""
Compute an optimal task policy for the reduced FCDT-MDP.
"""

import numpy as np                                                              # numerical arrays

def abm_dp(mdp_chi):                                                            # finite-horizon optimal policy
    """
    Apply backward induction to the reduced FCDT-MDP with gamma = 1.

    Input
        mdp_chi : SimpleNamespace() object with required attributes:
            .n   : positive integer horizon
            .A   : ordered task-action array containing one and two
            .k   : integer successor indices, one column per task action
            .r_a : parameter-independent accuracy reward array
            .r_b : parameter-independent imbalance reward array
            .r_s : parameter-independent switching reward array
            .β   : finite signed imbalance cost parameter
            .σ   : nonnegative finite switching cost parameter

    Output
        mdp_chi : input SimpleNamespace with the new attributes:
            .ρ   : reward array for the supplied cost parameters
            .d   : uint8 decision rules representing the optimal policy pi*
            .v   : optimal values at stage zero for all reduced states
            .qχ : action values indexed by stage, state row, and task action

    [Additional documentation]
    """
    # numerical arrays
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    n                  = int(mdp_chi.n)                                         # number of decision stages
    n_s                = mdp_chi.k.shape[0]                                     # reduced state-space cardinality
    mdp_chi.ρ          = np.full((n_s, 2), np.nan, dtype = np.float64)          # ρ_χ(s,a)
    mdp_chi.d          = np.zeros((n, n_s), dtype = np.uint8)                   # decision rules d_t*(s) representing π*
    mdp_chi.v          = np.full(n_s, np.nan, dtype = np.float64)               # first state-value buffer

    # reward evaluation
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    mdp_chi.ρ[:]      = (mdp_chi.r_a + mdp_chi.β * mdp_chi.r_b                  # accuracy and imbalance rewards
                         + mdp_chi.σ * mdp_chi.r_s)                             # switching reward

    # terminal initialization
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    mdp_chi.v[:]      = 0.0                                                     # v_n(s) = 0 for every state
    v_tt             = mdp_chi.v                                                # continuation values v_{t+1}
    v_t              = np.full(n_s, np.nan, dtype = np.float64)                 # local buffer for current state values v_t
    mdp_chi.qχ       = np.empty((n, n_s, 2), dtype = np.float64)                # reduced action values q_{χ,t}*(s,a)
    q_t               = np.full((n_s, 2), np.nan, dtype = np.float64)           # current action values q_{χ,t}*(s,a)

    # backward induction
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    for t in range(n - 1, -1, -1):                                              # stages n-1 through zero
        q_t[:]        = mdp_chi.ρ + v_tt[mdp_chi.k]                             # q_{χ,t}*(s,a) = ρ_χ(s,a) + v_{t+1}*(s_{t+1})
        mdp_chi.qχ[t] = q_t                                                     # retain current reduced action values
        mdp_chi.d[t] = mdp_chi.A[np.argmax(q_t, axis = 1)]                      # optimal decision function values d_t*(s)
        v_t[:]        = np.max(q_t, axis = 1)                                   # v_t*(s) = max_a q_t*(s,a)
        v_tt, v_t     = v_t, v_tt                                               # exchange buffers for the preceding stage

    # output
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    mdp_chi.v        = v_tt                                                     # optimal state values v_0
    return mdp_chi                                                              # optimal policy π* represented by .d, and values

Relation of the code to the optimal-policy theorem

The function abm_dp implements the construction in Theorem 9, “Existence and construction of an
optimal policy,” of the Supplementary Information for the reduced FCDT-MDP. The theorem’s horizon is
set to 𝑛 and its discount factor to 𝛾 = 1, so the objective is the expected sum of rewards over the remaining
decision stages. The function receives the reduced-model arrays prepared by abm_mdp_chi, together
with the parameters 𝛽 and 𝜎. It computes the optimal policy for this reduced model. Establishing that
the policy lifted to the original FCDT-MDP is also optimal requires the separate equivalence proof.

The rows of the numerical arrays enumerate states rather than time points. There are |𝑆𝜒 | = 4𝑛 + 3
such rows, including the initial state in row zero. The two columns of the reward and action-value arrays
correspond to the two actions in mdp_chi.A. The array mdp_chi.d additionally has a stage dimension,
because the optimal decision function may depend on the number of actions remaining. Its shape is
therefore 𝑛 by |𝑆𝜒 |. There is no decision-function row for stage 𝑛, at which no further action occurs.

The estimation domain allows a finite signed imbalance parameter and a finite nonnegative switching-cost
parameter. The reward calculation combines the three precomputed arrays according to

                                𝜌𝜒 (𝑠, 𝑎) = 𝑟𝑎 (𝑠, 𝑎) + 𝛽𝑟𝑏 (𝑠, 𝑎) + 𝜎𝑟𝑠 (𝑠, 𝑎).                       (168)

is time homogeneous. The state space, successor indices, and parameter-independent reward components
can remain unchanged when evaluating another parameter pair.

Mapping a reduced state to its array row

To evaluate the optimal decision function, A1 first extracts the reduced state 𝜒(𝑠𝑡 ) = (𝑠3𝑡 , 𝑠4𝑡 ) from the
full observed state. It then determines the row of this pair in the reduced state array. The builder places
the initial state in row zero, followed by all states with previous task 1 and then all states with previous
task 2. Within each task group, the imbalance increases from −𝑛 to 𝑛. Each group therefore contains
2𝑛 + 1 rows. Python uses zero-based array indices.
